# The 3 As of Cybersecurity: Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting

In cybersecurity, the "3 As" refer to three foundational concepts that work together to control access to systems and data: **Authentication**, **Authorization**, and **Accounting**. Together they form the core of access control, ensuring that the right people can access the right resources at the right time — and that their actions can be traced afterward.

## 1. Authentication

Authentication answers the question: *"Who are you?"* It is the process of verifying a user's or system's identity before granting access.

Authentication typically relies on one or more of three factors:

- **Something you know** — a password, PIN, or passphrase.
- **Something you have** — a smart card, hardware token, or authenticator app generating one-time codes.
- **Something you are** — a fingerprint, facial scan, or other biometric characteristic.

When two or more factors are combined, it is called **multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, which is one of the most effective defenses against credential theft and account takeover. Common authentication protocols include passwords, Kerberos, SAML, OAuth 2.0, and OpenID Connect.

The key point is that authentication only establishes identity; by itself, it does not determine what that identity is allowed to do.

## 2. Authorization

Authorization answers the question: *"What are you allowed to do?"* Once an identity has been verified, authorization determines which resources, actions, and data the user may access.

Authorization is commonly implemented through models such as:

- **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)** — permissions are assigned to roles, and users are assigned to roles.
- **Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)** — access decisions are based on attributes of the user, resource, and environment.
- **Mandatory Access Control (MAC)** — the system enforces access rules based on security labels.
- **Discretionary Access Control (DAC)** — resource owners grant access at their discretion.

A critical security principle here is the **principle of least privilege**: users should be granted only the minimum access necessary to perform their jobs. This limits the damage an attacker — or a careless insider — can cause.

## 3. Accounting

Accounting (sometimes called **Auditing**) answers the question: *"What did you do?"* It involves logging, monitoring, and recording user activity so that actions can be reviewed, attributed, and investigated.

Accounting usually covers:

- **Audit logs** — records of logins, access attempts, file changes, and administrative actions.
- **Monitoring and alerting** — real-time detection of suspicious or anomalous behavior.
- **Forensics and compliance** — evidence for incident response, legal investigations, and regulatory requirements such as HIPAA, PCI DSS, or SOX.

Without accounting, an organization may never know that a breach occurred, how it happened, or which accounts were compromised. Good accounting practices rely on accurate timestamps, tamper-resistant log storage, and centralized log management.

## How the 3 As Work Together

The three concepts form a logical sequence:

1. **Authentication** verifies identity ("Who are you?").
2. **Authorization** grants the appropriate level of access ("What can you do?").
3. **Accounting** records what occurred ("What did you do?").

For example, when an employee logs into a payroll system with a password and an MFA code, that is authentication. The system then checks whether that employee is allowed to view only their own payslips or also process payroll for others — that is authorization. Finally, the system logs when the employee logged in, what records they viewed or changed, and from which IP address — that is accounting.

## Why the 3 As Matter

Weakness in any one of the three undermines the others:

- Strong authorization is meaningless if authentication is compromised.
- Strong authentication cannot protect data if authorization is overly permissive.
- Neither matters for incident response if accounting is missing or incomplete.

Implementing the 3 As properly helps organizations protect sensitive data, meet compliance obligations, detect and respond to threats, and maintain trust with users and customers. They are, in short, the backbone of any sound security program.
