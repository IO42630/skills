# The 3 As of Cybersecurity

The "3 As" of cybersecurity is a shorthand for the three foundational pillars of access control: **Authentication**, **Authorization**, and **Accounting**. Together, they answer three distinct questions:

| Pillar | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Authentication | Who are you? |
| Authorization | What are you allowed to do? |
| Accounting | What did you actually do? |

## 1. Authentication

Authentication is the process of verifying that a user, device, or system is who or what it claims to be. It establishes identity before any access decision is made.

Common authentication factors fall into three categories:

- **Something you know** — passwords, PINs, security questions.
- **Something you have** — hardware tokens, smart cards, one-time-password apps.
- **Something you are** — fingerprints, facial recognition, iris scans.

Using two or more of these factors is called **multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, which significantly reduces the risk of credential theft. Weak or reused passwords remain one of the most common attack vectors, so authentication is often the first line of defense.

## 2. Authorization

Once identity is verified, authorization determines what that identity is permitted to do. It enforces the principle of **least privilege**: users should receive only the minimum access necessary to perform their role, and nothing more.

Authorization is typically implemented through:

- **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)** — permissions assigned to roles, and users assigned to roles.
- **Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)** — policies evaluated from attributes such as department, time, or location.
- **Access Control Lists (ACLs)** — explicit permissions attached to resources.

Authentication without authorization is insufficient: proving who you are says nothing about what you should be allowed to touch.

## 3. Accounting

Accounting (sometimes called **auditing**) is the recording and review of activity. It creates an audit trail that captures who did what, when, and where.

Its purposes include:

- **Auditability** — providing evidence for compliance and forensic investigations.
- **Non-repudiation** — users cannot credibly deny actions tied to their identity.
- **Monitoring and detection** — identifying suspicious behavior, such as unusual login times or privilege escalation attempts.

Typical accounting artifacts include system logs, authentication records, and change histories. These records are only useful if they are protected from tampering and reviewed regularly.

## Why the 3 As Work Together

The three As form a sequence:

1. **Authentication** proves identity.
2. **Authorization** grants the appropriate level of access.
3. **Accounting** records what happens with that access.

Weakening any one pillar undermines the others. Strong authentication means little if authorization is overly permissive, and even tight access control fails without logs to detect and investigate misuse. In frameworks such as **AAA** (Authentication, Authorization, Accounting), used widely in network access and remote-access systems, all three are treated as a single, integrated security function.

## Summary

The 3 As provide a simple mental model for access control: verify the user, limit what they can do, and record what they did. Applying all three consistently is a baseline requirement for secure systems, regulatory compliance, and effective incident response.
