# The 3 As of Cybersecurity: Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting

In cybersecurity, the "3 As" refer to **Authentication**, **Authorization**, and **Accounting**. Together they form the AAA framework, a foundational model for controlling access to systems, networks, and data. The three components work in sequence: first verify who someone is, then determine what they are allowed to do, and finally record what they actually did.

## 1. Authentication

Authentication is the process of verifying that a user, device, or service is who or what it claims to be. It answers the question: **"Who are you?"**

Authentication typically relies on one or more of three factors:

- **Something you know** — a password, PIN, or passphrase.
- **Something you have** — a hardware token, smart card, or authenticator app.
- **Something you are** — a fingerprint, facial scan, or other biometric trait.

When two or more of these factors are combined, it is called **multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, which is significantly stronger than any single factor. Common authentication technologies include passwords, one-time codes, digital certificates, and protocols such as RADIUS, Kerberos, and SAML.

The goal of authentication is to prevent unauthorized parties from impersonating legitimate users.

## 2. Authorization

Authorization determines what an authenticated identity is permitted to do. It answers the question: **"What are you allowed to access?"**

Authorization happens after authentication succeeds. It is enforced through policies, roles, and permissions. Common models include:

- **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)** — permissions are assigned to roles, and users are assigned to roles.
- **Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)** — access decisions are based on attributes such as department, time of day, or location.
- **Mandatory Access Control (MAC)** — the system enforces strict, administrator-defined rules.
- **Discretionary Access Control (DAC)** — resource owners decide who gets access.

The core principle is **least privilege**: users should receive only the minimum access needed to perform their duties. Good authorization prevents authenticated users from reaching resources, data, or actions beyond their scope.

## 3. Accounting

Accounting (also called **auditing**) tracks and records what users and systems do. It answers the question: **"What did you do?"**

Accounting involves logging events such as logins, logouts, file access, configuration changes, and failed access attempts. These records support:

- **Accountability** — actions can be traced back to a specific identity.
- **Forensics and incident response** — investigators can reconstruct what happened during a breach.
- **Compliance** — regulations such as HIPAA, PCI DSS, and SOX require audit trails.
- **Monitoring and detection** — unusual activity can trigger alerts.

Without accounting, an organization may know that access controls exist but have no evidence of whether they were used correctly or abused.

## How the Three Work Together

The 3 As are complementary and mutually reinforcing:

1. **Authentication** establishes identity with reasonable confidence.
2. **Authorization** limits what that identity can do.
3. **Accounting** records what was done, creating a trail for review and accountability.

If any one is missing, security weakens. Strong authentication without authorization lets users roam too freely. Authorization without accounting leaves misuse invisible. Accounting without authentication produces logs that cannot be tied to a responsible party.

## Conclusion

The AAA framework is a cornerstone of access control in modern cybersecurity. Authentication verifies identity, authorization enforces permissions, and accounting provides visibility and accountability. Implementing all three consistently — alongside practices like least privilege and multi-factor authentication — gives organizations a solid foundation for protecting their systems and data.
